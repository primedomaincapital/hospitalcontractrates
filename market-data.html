Hospital Contract Rates: Negotiated Prices, and Why They Converge

Since 2021, hospitals have had to publish the rates they negotiate with insurers. The files are enormous, inconsistent, and hard to read — which is why most summaries of them are either hype or guesswork. This page sticks to what published studies of those files actually found: rates vary widely, cash prices are often lower than negotiated ones, and transparency appears to be pulling rates toward the middle rather than down.

Methodology

Included: peer-reviewed and published studies that analyzed hospitals' own machine-readable transparency files (aggregated by Turquoise Health), plus the CMS rule changes that govern what those files must contain from 2026. Excluded: any single hospital's file quoted as a national rate; drug-price comparisons from transparency files, which a 2025 study found unusable because units are inconsistently reported; and insurer-specific rates for named hospitals, which change by contract and would mislead as benchmarks. Study periods are stated because transparency data before 2021 does not exist.

What the numbers mean

The convergence finding is the uncomfortable one. Transparency was sold as a way to push prices down. What Turquoise Health found between 2021 and 2024 was convergence: high rates fell 6.3% a year, but low rates rose 3.4% a year, and 83% of markets moved toward the middle. Low-priced hospitals, once they could see what rivals charged, negotiated up. That does not make transparency useless — a shopper comparing a $4,432 shoulder arthroscopy in a network facility with $2,643 at an independent one still has a real choice — but it does mean the average bill is not the number to watch. The spread is.
