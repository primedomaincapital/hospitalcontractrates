How Long Does Hospital Procurement Take From RFP to Signed Contract?

Three to nine months is the honest answer for a hospital RFP — and twelve to twenty-four for a new GPO contract. The delay is rarely the paperwork.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 2026

Terry Coutsolioutsos has sold medical devices into hospitals for years, and his advice to founders is blunt: plan for months, not weeks. Even a breakthrough technology needs about 90 days to get through a hospital's value analysis process, he wrote in November 2026. A common product in a crowded category can take 12 to 18 months. A disposable device priced around $1,000 can take six to eight months, sometimes a year. His bleakest line is the one vendors quote back to each other: you can have FDA clearance in hand and still wait a year to get the device on the shelf.

That is the honest shape of hospital procurement. It is not a purchase order. It is a gauntlet of committees, each meeting on its own calendar, each with veto power, each convinced its review is the one that cannot be rushed. The question vendors and hospital executives both ask — how long from RFP to signed contract — has an answer, but the answer is a range, and the range is wide enough to break a startup's cash forecast.

The clock, phase by phase

For a formal hospital RFP, three to nine months from release to contract execution is the range most health systems recognize, according to sourcing firm Zycus — and complex clinical technology sits at the upper end, with enterprise clinical systems able to exceed a year once implementation scoping is folded in. The elapsed time is not working time. Five reviews run one after another: clinical evaluation, value analysis committee approval, IT security, finance, and legal. Each takes days of actual work. The calendar is consumed by the sequence between them — a two-hour evaluation waiting three weeks for a two-hour window.

The group purchasing track is longer. Securing a healthcare GPO contract typically takes 12 to 24 months, according to Strategic GPO, broken into four phases: a pre-solicitation period of three to six months, a formal RFP process of six to nine months, contract adjudication of three to four months, and member adoption that runs six months or more after award. Vizient, the largest GPO in the United States, runs new-vendor categories through a process that typically takes 12 to 24 months from initial submission to contract award. For a new vendor in a genuinely new category, the full journey from first submission to a hospital actually buying the product runs 12 to 36 months, according to Provyx's analysis of GPO and value analysis data — and a submission rejected for thin clinical evidence can close the door for 12 to 18 months.

The value analysis committee is where most of that time goes. The committee — supply chain, nursing leadership, a physician champion, finance, and operations — weighs clinical benefit against financial impact and standardization goals. Review typically takes four to twelve weeks depending on meeting cadence, and most committees meet monthly. A submission that misses a meeting waits for the next one; a submission that arrives incomplete is deferred to the one after that. Two incomplete submissions can cost more elapsed time than the entire evaluation that preceded them.

The committee that sets the pace

The value analysis committee exists for a sound reason: hospitals need a systematic way to judge new products, control costs, and keep the formulary from sprawling into a warehouse of one-surgeon preferences. But its structure is what makes it slow. It meets on a fixed schedule rather than on demand. It requires a clinical evidence package, a financial analysis against the incumbent's price, a workflow impact assessment, and often an infection control review — and sometimes a pilot proposal — before it will even schedule a vote. Each of those documents has an owner, and the owners do not report to the vendor.

The committee has also changed character. Coutsolioutsos notes that doctors no longer sit on many of these committees, so a vendor's clinical champion is often not even in the room when the vote happens. McKinsey surveyed 150 U.S. physicians in September 2024 and found fewer than half reported their health system even had a value analysis committee or equivalent body — a finding that says as much about the opacity of the process as about its prevalence. The people selling into the system cannot see the decision-maker, and the decision-maker meets once a month.

The scale of the decision-making body compounds the delay. The Healthcare Sales Association's benchmark study puts the average healthcare sales cycle at 14.7 months with 22 decision-makers touching the deal before ink hits paper — a vendor-side benchmark, not a hospital-published figure, and it should be read as such. Software deals average roughly 12 months with about nine decision-makers; a $10,000 clinic software sale can close in 60 to 90 days; an enterprise electronic health record takes two years. Capital equipment routinely takes one to three years, in part because the capital budget committee at many hospitals meets once a year, creating months of dead air between meaningful steps.

Budget season and the fiscal calendar

The calendar does the rest of the damage. Most hospitals run on a July 1 fiscal year, with budget planning in January through March and the RFP window opening July through October. A vendor that arrives in November with a product that needs capital funding may wait eight months for the next budget cycle to begin — not because anyone objected, but because the money is allocated once a year. That is dead time no amount of sales effort recovers, and it is why the best engagement window, by the industry's own reckoning, runs January through May, before the budget locks.

There is some evidence the cycle is shortening. Menlo Ventures surveyed more than 700 healthcare executives and found health systems shortened average buying cycles from 8.0 to 6.6 months — 18 percent faster. The improvement is real but modest, and it is concentrated in software and commodity categories where the clinical risk is low. For physician-preference devices and enterprise systems, the committee calendar still rules.

The counterargument: the delay is a feature

It is easy to caricature this process as bureaucracy, and vendors do. But the committees exist because the alternative was worse. Before value analysis, a hospital's purchasing was often whatever its most persuasive surgeon preferred, and the result was exactly the price variation documented elsewhere on this site: the same knee implant at 2.1 times the price from one hospital to the next, driven by fragmented, surgeon-by-surgeon buying. The committee is the mechanism that forces a hospital to buy as one buyer instead of fifty. Slowness is the price of that discipline.

Patient safety is the stronger defense. A new implant, a new sterilization workflow, a new infusion pump — each carries failure modes that only surface in clinical evaluation, infection control review, and pilot use. A procurement process that could be completed in six weeks would be a process that had skipped the reviews that catch the device that migrates, the tray that does not fit the sterilizer, the software that does not talk to the electronic record. The 90-day floor Coutsolioutsos cites for breakthrough technology is not red tape. It is the minimum time to find out whether the thing works in this building, on these patients, with these people.

The defense has limits, and vendors are right to name them. A committee that meets monthly and defers incomplete submissions twice can burn a quarter on paperwork quality rather than clinical judgment. A capital cycle that allocates money once a year punishes innovation that arrives in the wrong month. And a process in which the physician champion is absent from the vote invites decisions made on spreadsheets rather than outcomes. The delay is defensible as a safety mechanism. It is harder to defend as a scheduling mechanism.

What this means for buyers and sellers

For vendors, the practical lesson is sequencing. Coutsolioutsos starts value analysis applications around day 31 of a launch, while the soft launch is still running — treating the committee as a parallel track, not a final gate. The firms that survive the cycle run IT security, finance, and legal reviews concurrently with clinical evaluation instead of waiting for the committee's blessing to start them. They also learn the budget calendar: a product that needs capital funding should be in front of the hospital in January, not November.

For hospitals, the implication runs the other way. Every month a procurement cycle runs long is a month the hospital pays the incumbent's price — and if the incumbent's price is the one at the 90th percentile, the delay has a dollar value. The Menlo Ventures finding that cycles shortened 18 percent suggests the process can move faster without breaking. The hospitals that moved fastest did not abolish their committees. They made the committees meet more often, accept rolling submissions, and decide on complete packages the first time.

For the buyer of contract-rate data, the timeline is the reason the data matters. A procurement cycle that takes nine months to run and three years at the GPO level is a cycle in which the negotiating leverage sits with whoever knows the going rate. The hospital that walks into value analysis with the 10th-percentile price in hand negotiates from evidence. The hospital that walks in with last year's invoice negotiates from memory. The clock will not get shorter on its own. The information inside the room can still get better.
