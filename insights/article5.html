The April 1 Deadline: What the 2026 Transparency Rule Actually Changed

Under the 2026 rule, every hospital price file must carry an attestation signed by a senior officer. Transparency stopped being a compliance exercise and became a personal accountability event.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 2026

The signature was the point. Under the hospital price transparency rule as it stood from January 1, 2026, every hospital's machine-readable file of standard charges must carry an attestation signed by a senior officer — the chief executive, the president, or a designated senior official — stating that the information is true, accurate, and complete. Somewhere in a hospital system this spring, an executive signed a document attesting to prices their own contracting team could not fully explain. That signature is the story of 2026 in hospital contracting: transparency stopped being a compliance exercise and became a personal accountability event.

What the 2026 rule actually requires

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services finalized the CY 2026 rule on November 21, 2025, with enforcement beginning April 1, 2026. The core change is simple to state and hard to execute. Since January 2021, hospitals have had to post a machine-readable file of standard charges, including the price negotiated with each insurer and the cash price, plus a consumer display of 300 shoppable services. The problem was that many negotiated prices were expressed as percentages or formulas rather than dollars — "120 percent of Medicare," say — which told a patient nothing. An executive order on February 25, 2025 demanded the actual prices of items and services, not estimates. The 2026 rule delivers on that demand: wherever a negotiated price is a percentage or a formula, the hospital must now also publish the median and the 10th and 90th percentile of what that insurer actually paid, drawn from remittance data, along with the count of allowed amounts. The estimated-allowed-amount field is gone. In its place: real paid amounts, in dollars, with a named officer attesting to them.

Enforcement arrived quickly. Between April and early June 2026, 519 hospitals received noncompliance letters, according to Forvis Mazars. On October 5, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission warned 24 of the largest health care companies that the CMS rules "do not create a safe harbor from FTC Act liability" for incomplete price disclosures — a message aimed squarely at systems that had treated transparency as a paperwork exercise. CMS has issued 27 civil monetary penalties to date, and 20 hospitals have appealed them to an administrative law judge. The proposed rule offers a 35 percent penalty reduction for hospitals that waive their right to an ALJ hearing. Compliance is improving: the advocacy group that audits the files found 49.4 percent of 2,000 hospitals fully compliant in September 2026, its highest reading yet — which is also a way of saying half of American hospitals still are not.

Why the percentile data changes negotiations

The median and percentile figures matter because they expose what the negotiated rate hides. Two hospitals can report the same contracted percentage and be paid wildly different dollars, because the percentage applies to different base rates, different service mixes, and different patient populations. The 10th-to-90th spread is the honest picture of what a payer actually pays, and it is now public. That is why the data has become a product: Turquoise Health, which collects hospital and payer transparency files and turns them into searchable negotiated-rate data, raised a $40 million Series C in March 2026. Trilliant Health sells a Rates product alongside its demand and supply analytics. Sage Transparency, built by an Indiana employers' coalition with the RAND Corporation's price studies, shows each hospital's prices as a percentage of Medicare, for free. Employers, who ultimately pay the bills, are the fastest-growing users of this data — and they are using it to buy smarter, not just to look.

This site has covered the scale of the variation before: the same inpatient procedure varying 9.1-to-1 across the country, coronary bypass from $27,683 to $247,902. The 2026 rule does not fix that variation. It makes it undeniable, in dollars, with a signature on it.

The counterargument: disclosure is not the same as competition

Transparency's skeptics have a fair point. Five years into mandatory disclosure, negotiated prices have not converged, patients rarely shop for hospital care, and the files — even improved — remain hard for ordinary people to use. The value of the data accrues mostly to employers, analysts, and rival hospitals, not to the patient choosing where to get surgery. And the compliance burden falls hardest on small and rural hospitals, which lack the contracting teams to produce clean files and the leverage to negotiate better rates whatever the file says. A rule that fines a 25-bed hospital for a bad spreadsheet while a large system absorbs the same fine as a rounding error is not obviously a rule that makes care cheaper.

There is also the question of what the percentile data will do to negotiations themselves. Payers can now see exactly what their competitors pay at every hospital. In some markets that will compress rates toward the median. In others it may set a floor — a payer that was paying at the 10th percentile now knows it can be pushed toward the middle. Transparency cuts both ways, and the direction depends on who has more leverage in the room.

What this means for hospitals and for buyers of care

For hospitals, the implication is operational: audit the machine-readable file before the next enforcement letter arrives, and treat the attestation as what it is — a statement a named executive is personally accountable for. The hospitals that struggled most in 2026 were not the ones with the highest prices. They were the ones whose files did not match their remittance data. For employers and purchasers, the implication is the opposite: the percentile data is the negotiating lever that did not exist two years ago. A contract negotiated against the 90th percentile, when the median is public, is a contract negotiated badly. The rule did not make hospital prices fair. It made them visible, in dollars, with a signature — and visibility, over time, is what makes prices move.
