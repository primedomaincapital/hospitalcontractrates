Device pricing

What Do Hospitals Actually Pay for Common Medical Devices?

For the same defibrillator model, one hospital paid $8,723 more than another. The spread is not a rounding error — it is the business model.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 2026

The Government Accountability Office asked sixty hospitals a simple question: what did you pay for the implant? Four of them could not answer at all. Their contracts forbade it. The confidentiality clauses in their purchase agreements barred them from telling a federal auditor the price of a device already inside a patient. That was 2012, and the answer the GAO did get was worse than silence. For the same model of implantable defibrillator, the gap between the lowest price one hospital reported and the highest another reported was $8,723. The median prices across four defibrillator models ran from $16,445 to $19,007 — and those were medians, not outliers.

Fourteen years later, the question is still open, and the spread is still the story. Hospitals do not pay a market price for medical devices. They pay a negotiated price, set in private, tiered by volume, layered with rebates, and guarded by contract language that treats the number itself as proprietary. The result is a market where the identical box, made by the same manufacturer, on the same truck, can cost one hospital nearly twice what it costs another forty miles away.

The implant spread, in dollars

Start with the joints, because the data there is the cleanest. James C. Robinson of the University of California, Berkeley, and his colleagues examined 10,155 knee replacements and 5,013 hip replacements across 61 hospitals in 2008, and published the results in the Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery in 2012. The average implant cost per case ranged from $1,797 to $12,093 for knees and from $2,392 to $12,651 for hips. The device alone accounted for anywhere from 13 percent to 87 percent of the total cost of a knee replacement. Patient characteristics explained almost none of it: 2.5 percent of the variation in knee implant costs, 4.4 percent for hips. Hospital characteristics explained 61 percent for knees and 36 percent for hips. The rest — 36.5 percent for knees, 59.5 percent for hips — was variation inside individual hospitals that neither patient nor hospital traits could explain.

A follow-up study by Derek A. Haas, Kevin J. Bozic, Anthony M. DiGioia, Zirui Song, and Robert S. Kaplan, published in the Journal of Arthroplasty in 2017, put the spread in terms a purchasing director can use. Across 27 high-volume hospitals, the hospital at the 90th percentile paid 2.1 times as much for a knee implant as the hospital at the 10th percentile, and 1.7 times as much for a hip implant. The single strongest predictor of a low price was not size. It was governance: hospitals where a joint committee of administrators and surgeons chose vendors and negotiated together paid 17 percent less for knee implants and 23 percent less for hip implants than hospitals that left the decision to one side or the other.

Cardiac devices tell the same story at higher dollar values. The GAO's 2012 report, requested by Senate Finance Committee chairman Max Baucus, found that Medicare spending on procedures involving implantable devices rose from $16.1 billion in fiscal 2004 to $19.8 billion in fiscal 2009 — 4.3 percent a year, in line with other hospital spending. But the prices underneath that total were anything but uniform. Across 31 hospitals reporting, the spread between the lowest and highest price paid for a single defibrillator model ran from $6,844 to $8,723 depending on the model. One hospital paid 83 percent more than another for the exact same knee implant. The GAO's conclusion was blunt: the lack of transparency raised questions about whether hospitals were achieving the best prices possible.

Why the same box costs different hospitals different money

Four forces set the price, and none of them is the cost of manufacturing the device. The first is the surgeon. In orthopedics and cardiology, the physician who implants the device usually chooses it, and surgeons develop loyalties to particular systems — Stryker, Zimmer Biomet, DePuy Synthes, Smith+Nephew — built over years of training, instrumentation, and operating-room routine. A hospital that wants to switch vendors to save 20 percent must persuade the people who do the operating to relearn their tools. That is a clinical negotiation before it is a commercial one, and it is why physician-preference items resist the commoditization that swept gloves, gowns, and syringes years ago.

The second force is secrecy itself. The GAO found that all five manufacturers, all five group purchasing organizations, all three hospital systems, and one of two small hospitals it interviewed reported that all or almost all of their contracts restricted disclosure of pricing information to some extent. A hospital that cannot tell its own surgeons what a device costs — four hospitals told the GAO they could not even share prices internally because of confidentiality clauses — cannot run a rational purchasing process. Secrecy is not a side effect of this market. It is the mechanism that keeps prices apart.

The third is scale, and it cuts both ways. Large systems negotiate from volume; a 120-bed community hospital buys a few dozen hips a year and takes the price it is given. But scale alone does not explain the spread. The Robinson study found that within-hospital variation — the same hospital paying different prices for similar implants — accounted for more than a third of the total variance for knees and nearly three-fifths for hips. That points to fragmented buying inside the building: different surgeons, different vendors, different deals, no single owner of the number.

The fourth is the rebate stack. Device contracts routinely bundle the implant price with loaner instrumentation, training, service, and volume rebates, so the invoice price is not the economic price. Two hospitals can report different implant costs and both be right, because one is counting the tray, the rep in the room, and the rebate check, and the other is not. Any benchmark that compares sticker prices without unwinding the bundle is comparing different things.

The scale of the spend makes the spread expensive. The American Hospital Association reported U.S. hospitals spent $146.9 billion on medical supplies in 2023, up $6.6 billion in a single year, plus $115 billion on drugs. Implants are the dearest corner of that spend: a hip implant that cost about $350 to manufacture was billed to hospitals at $4,500 to $7,500 in 2013 reporting by the New York Times — a dated figure, but the direction of travel since has been up, not down. Internationally, Health Affairs research showed U.S. hospitals paid up to six times what German hospitals paid for cardiac implants, with drug-eluting stents running roughly $1,000 above German prices every year from 2006 to 2014.

What the transparency files do — and do not — reveal

A reader of this site might assume the hospital price transparency rule solved this. It did not, and the reason matters. The machine-readable files hospitals must publish disclose standard charges: what the hospital charges payers and patients for items and services. They do not disclose what the hospital paid the vendor for the device. The purchase price — the number a supply-chain director actually negotiates — appears nowhere in the file. The transparency rule made the hospital's selling prices visible. Its buying prices remain private.

That gap is the commercial opening. Turquoise Health, which collects hospital and payer transparency files and turns them into searchable negotiated-rate data, raised a $40 million Series C in March 2026, led by Oak HC/FT with Andreessen Horowitz participating. Its pitch is that the disclosed rates, properly assembled, reveal what payers actually pay — and by extension, what leverage hospitals have. But even the best-assembled transparency data is a picture of the selling side. The buying side — what hospitals pay Medtronic, Boston Scientific, Abbott, and the orthopedic makers — is still reconstructed from studies, surveys, and the occasional leaked contract, not from a public dataset.

The counterargument: variation is not always waste

There is a defense of the spread, and it deserves a hearing. A device price is not a commodity price because the device is not sold alone. The hospital buying a knee system is buying the implant, the instrument trays, the rep who stands in the operating room, the training, the revision support, and the liability posture of a vendor that will answer the phone at 2 a.m. A hospital that pays more may be buying a thicker service wrapper, or a system its surgeons can implant faster, with fewer complications and shorter stays. On that view, comparing implant invoices without comparing outcomes is a category error — and the studies that document price variation generally do not measure whether the expensive implant produced a better knee.

Small hospitals have a second defense. A rural facility doing forty hips a year cannot credibly threaten to walk away from a vendor, cannot standardize across three surgeons who each want a different system, and cannot staff a value analysis team. Its high price is not a failure of negotiation. It is the price of being small in a market built for bulk. Telling that hospital to benchmark harder is telling it to solve with effort a problem created by structure.

Both points are real. Neither explains a 2.1-to-1 spread between high-volume hospitals buying the same implant, or a hospital paying 83 percent more than its neighbor for the identical knee. Service wrappers differ, but not by 83 percent. Volume differs, but the Robinson data shows the unexplained variance lives inside hospitals as much as between them. The defense explains the edges of the spread. It does not explain the middle.

What this means for buyers of care

For hospitals, the Haas finding is the actionable one. The 17 to 23 percent discount associated with a joint hospital-physician purchasing committee is the largest single lever in the published literature, larger than volume, larger than system-level contracting. It costs no capital and requires no new vendor. It requires the surgeons and the supply chain to sit at the same table and agree that price is a clinical variable, not an administrative afterthought. Hospitals that have not built that table are paying for the absence.

For employers and purchasers, the implication is narrower but still useful. The transparency files will not tell you what a hospital paid for a stent. They will tell you what the hospital charged every payer for the procedure that used the stent — and the spread in those charges, from $22,047 to $165,386 for the same stent procedure across 124 California hospitals in earlier research on this site, is a signal about the hospital's cost discipline, not just its pricing power. A hospital that cannot buy implants cheaply rarely sells procedures cheaply.

For the company that eventually owns this category, the gap is the product. Somebody will assemble the buying-side benchmark — purchase prices, net of rebates, by device, by hospital — the way Turquoise Health assembled the selling side. The data exists in fragments: in GPO contracts, in hospital cost reports, in the studies cited here. The firm that puts it together credibly, with the device identified and the bundle unwound, holds the reference rate for a $146.9 billion spend. The GAO asked the question in 2012. The market still has not answered it.
